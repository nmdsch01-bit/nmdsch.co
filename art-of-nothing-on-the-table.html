The Art of Nothing on the Table

On ma — the Japanese principle of intentional emptiness — and what a bare surface is actually for.

The Editors Sept 2026 7 min read

A friend visiting from Kyoto once stood in my living room and looked, for a long moment, at my coffee table. Not rudely — she was too polite for that — but carefully, the way you look at something you're trying to understand. On it sat a candle, a stack of unread magazines, a used mug, a phone charger coiled like a small animal, and a bowl of keys that had long since stopped being a bowl of keys and become a bowl of everything. "It looks comfortable," she said, which I understood, even then, was not quite a compliment.

Later that evening, without asking, she cleared it. Not into a drawer — just onto the floor beside it, temporarily, so I could see the table itself. Pale wood, a single knot near one corner, nothing on it at all. I remember feeling strangely exposed, as if the table had been undressed. And then, a few seconds later, something closer to relief.

The Japanese have a word for what she'd made visible: ma (間). It doesn't translate cleanly. The dictionary will tell you it means "space" or "interval," but that undersells it. Ma isn't the absence of something — it's a presence in its own right. The pause between two notes of music is not silence; it's what lets you hear the notes as separate, as themselves. The table, empty, wasn't waiting to be filled. It was doing something.

In traditional Japanese rooms, this shows up architecturally — a single scroll on an otherwise bare wall, one flower in an alcove built specifically to hold one flower. The emptiness isn't neglect. It's the most expensive part of the room, because it's the part that takes discipline to protect. Anyone can add an object. It takes a different kind of attention to look at a beautiful, empty surface and decide, on purpose, to leave it that way.

We tend to treat surfaces as problems waiting to be solved by objects. Ma suggests the opposite: that a surface might already be complete.

Western interiors, mine included, tend to run on a different logic. A bare surface reads as unfinished, even a little sad — evidence that you haven't gotten around to styling it yet. So we fill it: a tray to "corral" the clutter, a candle to justify the tray, a coaster to protect the surface from the mug that shouldn't be there in the first place. Each object is reasonable on its own. Together, they're a kind of noise, and most of us have simply stopped being able to hear it.

This is, I think, the real argument for buying fewer, better objects — not aesthetics, not minimalism as a look, but this: every object you put down is a decision about what gets to interrupt the quiet. A well-made table, the kind worth the price, isn't just a surface to hold things. It's a surface worth occasionally holding nothing.

My friend flew back to Kyoto a few days later. The magazines went back on the table almost immediately — I'm not, it turns out, a monk. But I moved the bowl of everything to a drawer, and I leave the table bare more often now, not because it looks better in photographs, but because every time I walk past it empty, I notice it. Which is, maybe, the entire point of a table you actually chose: not that it holds your things well, but that it's still worth looking at when it's holding nothing at all.
