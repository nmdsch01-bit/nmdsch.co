Wabi-Sabi

Growing Old Without Pretending Otherwise

A note on objects that don't fake newness — tarnished metal, yellowed paper, and the case against constant upgrading.

The Editors Sept 2026 6 min read

There is a brass door handle in my grandmother's house that has gone almost black in the middle, worn back to gold at the edges where decades of hands have missed the center by an inch. Nobody polishes it. Nobody has ever suggested replacing it. It is, without question, the most beautiful handle I have ever touched, and it looks nothing like it did the day it was installed.

We don't really have a good word in English for what's happened to it. "Worn" sounds like damage. "Aged" sounds passive, like something done to the object rather than something the object did. The Japanese come closer with wabi-sabi — not a style, despite how often it's sold as one, but a way of seeing beauty in things that are incomplete, impermanent, and visibly used. A cracked bowl repaired with gold lacquer, kintsugi, isn't hiding its break. It's arguing that the break is now part of the object's story, and arguably its most interesting chapter.

Compare that to the logic most of our objects are designed under: newness as the default state to be defended. A phone case exists to keep the phone looking unused. A screen protector protects the protector. We've built an entire retail category — the "like new" market — around the idea that an object's highest value is the moment just before anyone has touched it, and everything after that is decline.

An object designed to resist aging is making you a promise it can't keep. Sooner or later, it loses — and then it just looks broken, instead of lived-in.

This is where cheaply made things and well-made things quietly part ways. A laminate desk doesn't age; it degrades. The finish chips, the edges swell if they meet water, and there's no point at ten years where it looks better than it did at one. It was only ever moving toward failure. Solid wood does something else entirely — it darkens, the grain gets more pronounced, small dents catch the light differently than the surrounding surface. It was designed, whether the maker said so or not, with the assumption that time would be part of the finish.

That's really the test I've started applying before buying anything meant to last: not "how does this look now," but "what does this look like in the middle, when it's neither new nor an heirloom, just an object that's clearly been used for a while." Cheap things are at their best on day one. Good things are often at their best on day one thousand.

I'm not arguing for neglect — a well-cared-for object still needs care, and there's a difference between patina and simply letting something fall apart. But somewhere between the two is a kind of permission we've mostly forgotten to give ourselves: to let a good thing look like what it is. Used. Chosen once, and then lived with, rather than replaced the moment it stops looking like the photograph it was bought from.

My grandmother's door handle will outlive me. It will keep darkening in the middle, keep going gold at the edges, and at no point will it look new again — nor, I suspect, would she want it to.
